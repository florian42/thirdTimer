# What the research says about focus and breaks

This note records the evidence behind Third Timer's break suggestions. It was
gathered in November 2026 from abstracts and secondary descriptions of the
sources below; the full texts were mostly not read. Treat effect sizes as
reported by the abstracts.

## Summary

- **There is no proven "natural" work interval.** Sustained attention on
  monotonous lab tasks drops within 15 to 30 minutes, and mental fatigue on
  demanding tasks builds over one to three hours. Nothing pins down one number.
- **Breaks mainly help how people feel.** Short breaks raise energy and lower
  fatigue by small amounts. Their effect on output is small or absent for
  demanding work, and larger for longer breaks.
- **People left to themselves break later and end up more tired.** That
  supports a suggestion for someone who pushes through.
- **Earlier, more frequent breaks restore more** than breaks saved for later.
- **A prompt lands better at a natural pause** than in the middle of a task.
- **A low, slow, fading sound played once reads as least urgent.** Evidence
  for bells in particular is thin.

## What Third Timer does with it

| Decision | Basis | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First suggestion after 60 minutes of unbroken work, then every 30. Both adjustable. | Fatigue builds over 1 to 3 hours; self-regulated breakers wait too long. The exact figure is a guardrail, not a constant. | Moderate for nudging; the number is a judgement |
| Suggestions can be dismissed ("Not now") and never lock the screen. | Freely chosen breaks restore more; about half of prompted breaks are taken. | Moderate |
| The bell waits for a 15-second lull in typing, for at most 10 minutes. | Notifications at task breakpoints cause less frustration. Using idle time as the signal is our own choice. | Strong in the lab; untested for break prompts |
| One soft, low bell that fades, struck once. Two strikes end a break. | Urgency rises with pitch, speed, abrupt onset and repetition. | Strong for urgency; weak for bells |
| Lunch suggested at a set time, as a longer break. | Lunch activities chosen freely reduce fatigue; a walk or relaxation at lunch helps the afternoon. | Moderate |
| The break screen suggests leaving the screen and walking. | Walks and relaxation help; phone use in breaks is linked to exhaustion. | Moderate to weak |
| Time invested is shown; time left to a deadline is not. | Felt time pressure lowers creative thinking. Whether a countdown display causes it is untested. | Opinion |

## Claims that do not hold up

- **A 90-minute waking focus cycle.** Weak and contested (Kripke et al. 1977).
- **52 minutes of work and 17 of break.** An average from one company's app
  users, published on its blog; a later update gave 112 and 26.
- **25 and 5 (Pomodoro) as optimal.** In one small study it raised fatigue
  faster than self-chosen breaks (Smits et al. 2025).
- **23 minutes to refocus after an interruption.** From interviews, not a
  measured constant.
